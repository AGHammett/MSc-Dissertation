A predictive model is multicalibrated if its probability estimates are accurate (calibrated) within every
group of a specified collection of possibly overlapping subpopulations, such as patients grouped by
age, sex or condition; among cases given a probability of 70%, for instance, about 70% should occur. A
common way of summarising this is the largest calibration gap over the groups and over bands of
predicted probability, restricted to groups above a stated minimum size; this is the target of the
project. The collection of groups is taken as given and may be very large, with an extension to groups
defined by a rich function class. Because the largest of many estimated gaps is chosen using the same
data that estimate it, it is biased upwards, and a confidence interval (a range meant to contain the true
value with a stated probability, called its coverage) that ignores this selection may fall short of its
stated coverage. Two existing approaches address related problems. Bootstrap-based bounds that hold
for every group at once, with asymptotically exact coverage, have been developed for auditing model
performance over groups, with multicalibration as an application. Separately, inference for the largest
or smallest value of a collection of estimated quantities has been developed in econometrics, with a
selection step that shortens the interval. Both guarantees are asymptotic. This project asks, for the
worst-group calibration gap, how far an interval can be shortened below the one obtained from
bounds on every group at once, by using the fact that the target is a maximum and the structure of the
groups, such as nesting and overlap, while keeping its stated coverage. The outcome is a single result:
either such an interval, with a proof of its coverage and a comparison of its width with the all-groups
interval and with sample splitting (choosing the worst group on one part of the data and estimating its
error on the other), or a proof that, for a stated group structure, no valid interval is substantially
shorter. Deliverables may also include an open-source release of the proposed method. The work
combines proof and simulation, and suits a student with a strong grounding in probability and
asymptotic statistics and an interest in the statistical evaluation of machine-learning models.
