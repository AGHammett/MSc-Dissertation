# What Does a Human-Preference Leaderboard Actually Rank?: Overview

**Supervisor:** Professor Milan Vojnović · **Prepared:** 21 October 2026 · **Sources:** the brief and the 5 supplied papers only

> **Labels.** **[Paper]** a paper states it (with reference) · **[Brief]** the brief states it · **[Interpretation]** my own reading. Check it before relying on it.

## 1. The brief

**[Brief]** Leaderboards such as Chatbot Arena (now LMArena) rank large language models (LLMs) by asking users to vote between two anonymous model responses. They then pool the votes of a self-selected crowd into one score per model. A model's score therefore reflects how often it beats the models it happens to be shown against, averaged over voters. If voters disagree, the pooled ranking need not match what any particular group of users would conclude, and it can depend on which pairs of models were shown. The project asks what such a ranking actually measures and which ranking claims carry over to other user populations. It has three technical asks: (a) characterise what the pooled ranking estimates when voters are heterogeneous; (b) derive bounds on how a model's rank could change under different or uncertain mixes of users; (c) develop confidence statements of the form "model A ranks above model B for every plausible user mix". The methods are to be checked by simulation, applied to publicly released leaderboard votes using language and prompt type as observable user segments, and tested on a dataset in which the voter groups are known. The brief says the work combines theory, simulation and data analysis, and suits someone with a strong grounding in likelihood theory.

**Project type:** methodological with a substantial theoretical core, plus applied data analysis. **[Interpretation]** Asks (a)–(c) are statements about estimands and inference: what a misspecified maximum-likelihood fit converges to, and how to make inference uniform over a set of mixing weights. That makes the theory and the method the centre of gravity. The simulation and the two real-data analyses (Arena-type votes; PRISM, which records who voted) are validation and demonstration rather than the main contribution.

## 2. Paper by paper

**Recommended reading order and why.** (1) Chatbot Arena first: it defines the object being studied, i.e. the platform, the data and the exact estimand behind the ranking. (2) Ameli et al. next: same data, a classical paired-comparison viewpoint (likelihoods, ties, identifiability), and no heterogeneity. It is the "single-population" baseline. (3) Distributional Preference Learning (DPL): the one paper with a theorem about what a pooled Bradley–Terry fit estimates when there is unobserved heterogeneity. That is ask (a). (4) PRISM: the dataset with known voter groups, and empirical evidence that rankings move with the user mix (asks (b) and the "known groups" test). (5) Frauen et al. last: the most technically demanding paper (semiparametric efficiency, debiased ML). It supplies a general inference toolkit for ranking functionals, and it explicitly names partial identification as future work, which is close to asks (b)–(c).

### 2.1 Chatbot Arena (Chiang, Zheng, Sheng, Angelopoulos, Li, Li, Zhu, Zhang, Jordan, Gonzalez, Stoica, 2024)
arXiv:2403.04132v1 [cs.AI], 7 March 2024 (preprint as supplied; Ameli et al. cite it as ICML 2024).

- **Problem.** **[Paper]** Static, ground-truth benchmarks cannot capture open-ended, real-world use and can become contaminated. The authors want a live evaluation based on human preferences, plus statistically sound ways to turn pairwise votes into a ranking with uncertainty (§1, pp. 1–2).
- **Approach.** **[Paper]** Users type any prompt, see answers from two anonymous models, and vote (options include "tie" and "both are bad"); identities are revealed only after the vote (§3.1, p. 3). Formally, at time $t$ a pair $A_t$ is served and a response $H_t\in[0,1]$ observed (§4, p. 3). The ranking is defined through a score $s(\mathbb P)$ that depends on a joint distribution $\mathbb P$ over pairs and responses, with rank $=1+\sum_{m'}\mathbf 1\{s(\mathbb P)_{m'}>s(\mathbb P)_m\}$ (eq. 1, p. 4). The default score is the vector of *population* Bradley–Terry (BT) coefficients that minimise expected binary cross-entropy (eqs. 2–3, p. 4). The text says "by default, we will target a uniform distribution over $\mathcal A$" (p. 4), i.e. uniform over model pairs. The estimator is inverse-probability-reweighted MLE, with weights $1/P(A_t)$ so that the target is the uniform-over-pairs score despite non-uniform sampling (eq. 7, p. 5). Confidence intervals use the sandwich (Huber–White) variance, chosen over the pivot bootstrap after a simulation study (§5, p. 5; App. A). A chi-squared confidence set gives simultaneous coverage and an "approximate ranking" $R_m$ with $\mathbb P(\exists m: R_m>\mathrm{rank}(\mathbb P)_m)\le\alpha$ (eq. 8, p. 5). There is also an active pair-sampling rule (eq. 9) and an anomalous-user test based on exchangeability p-values (eq. 10, §5.1; proof in App. C).
- **Main findings and results.** **[Paper]** About 240K votes from about 90K users in more than 100 languages (77% English, 5% Chinese) by January 2024, over 50 models (§3.2, p. 3; Table 1). Topic modelling (BERTopic on prompt embeddings) finds 600 clusters with a long-tailed distribution (§6.1, p. 6). GPT-4's win rate against Llama-2-70b-chat ranges from 97% on coding and reasoning clusters to below 60% on others, judged by GPT-4-Turbo (§6.2, Table 2). So model strength is topic-dependent. Crowd–expert agreement is 72–83%, against 79–90% between experts (§6.3, Table 3). Simulated interval coverage is close to nominal (Fig. 6). Adaptive sampling saves 54% of samples for the win matrix but only 5% for the BT score (§7.1, p. 8). App. B defines a nonparametric BT score (eqs. 11–16) that recovers the BT coefficients when BT holds and stays valid under intransitivity or misspecification.
- **Key assumptions.** **[Paper]** Inference relies on a CLT for adaptively sampled data with $P_t(a)>\epsilon>0$ and $P_t(a)$ converging almost surely (p. 5, citing Durrett Thm 8.2.8). The BT model may be misspecified; the target is then the cross-entropy minimiser and the sandwich variance is used (p. 4). **[Interpretation]** Voters are treated as exchangeable draws from one population. The estimand $s(\mathbb P)$ is whatever the *current* voter population and prompt mix induce, and nothing in the paper conditions on who voted.
- **Relation to the other papers.** **[Interpretation]** This is the object of study. Equation (3) is exactly the "pooled ranking" of the brief. The authors' own limitations section anticipates the problem: the user base is likely "LLM hobbyists and researchers", which "may result in a biased distribution of users", and the prompt distribution may be skewed (§8, p. 8). Ask (a) amounts to characterising $s(\mathbb P)$ when $\mathbb P$ is a mixture over voter groups. DPL (2.3) gives part of the answer. Frauen et al. (2.5) generalise the estimand. Ameli et al. (2.2) refine the likelihood but keep a single population. PRISM (2.4) re-runs a comparison against Arena-type data.

### 2.2 A Statistical Framework for Ranking LLM-Based Chatbots (Ameli, Zhuang, Stoica, Mahoney, 2025)
arXiv:2412.18407v2 [stat.ML], 29 May 2025.

- **Problem.** **[Paper]** Arena-style ranking treats a tie as half a win and half a loss, and it models neither ties nor correlations between competitors. The likelihood also has symmetries that make the optimisation unstable (§1, pp. 1–2).
- **Approach.** **[Paper]** Input is aggregated counts $(G,W,T)$: a comparison graph, a win matrix and a tie matrix. The likelihood is a product of multinomials over compared pairs (eq. 1, §2.1). Models considered: BT (eq. 2), Rao–Kupper (eq. 4) and Davidson (eq. 5) tie models. The new elements are a *factored tie model*, with pair-specific tie thresholds $\eta_{ij}=g_i\cdot\phi_j+g_j\cdot\phi_i$ built from a DCT-IV basis (eqs. 6–8), and *Thurstonian* versions in which scores are random, $x=\mu+\epsilon$ with $\mathrm{cov}(\epsilon)=\Sigma=D+\Lambda\Lambda^\top$ (eqs. 9–13). Constraints remove three symmetries: score shift ($\sum x_i=0$), joint scaling of scores and covariance ($\mathrm{tr}(P\Sigma P)=1$, eq. 14) and translation of factor loadings ($\|\Lambda^\top\mathbf 1\|=0$, eq. 15) (§2.5). Fitting uses BFGS with analytic gradients.
- **Main findings and results.** **[Paper]** On a September 2024 Arena snapshot (129 models, 3,455 pairs, 1,374,996 comparisons: 43.3% wins, 36.2% losses, 20.4% ties; §3) the factored tie model cuts tie-fit error "by two orders of magnitude" (§1, §3.1, Fig. 1). Thirty model variants are compared (Table D.1). The top of the ranking is stable across models; lower ranks vary (§4.1, App. E). Whether covariance is included drives the clustering of rankings more than the tie modelling does (§4.1). App. B shows the Fisher information is singular under shift invariance (Prop. B.1), so only score *differences* are identifiable (B.3). Covariance $\Sigma$ is identifiable only up to an equivalence class, while the dissimilarity matrix $S$ is unique (Prop. C.1).
- **Key assumptions.** **[Paper]** Linear stochastic transitivity, $P_{i\succ j}=F(\zeta(x_i)-\zeta(x_j))$ (§2.2), and a connected comparison graph (§2.1). **[Interpretation]** The data are pooled counts, so prompt, language and voter information is discarded by construction. Heterogeneity can only appear indirectly, through the Thurstonian noise term.
- **Relation to the other papers.** **[Interpretation]** This paper shows how much the ranking depends on *modelling* choices (ties, covariance) with the population held fixed. The project asks the complementary question: how much it depends on the *population* with the model held fixed. Two technical points carry over directly. Rank statements should be built on identifiable contrasts (score differences). Ties need a deliberate treatment, because Arena data contain about 20% ties. Note one discrepancy: Ameli et al. say Arena "relies on the Elo rating system" (§1), while Chiang et al. say they switched from Elo to BT coefficients (p. 4).

### 2.3 Distributional Preference Learning: hidden context in RLHF (Siththaranjan, Laidlaw, Hadfield-Menell, 2024)
ICLR 2024; arXiv:2312.08358v2 [cs.LG], 17 April 2024.

- **Problem.** **[Paper]** Preference data depend on "hidden context", meaning variables that affect the comparison but are not inputs to the learned utility. Annotator identity, labelling objective and irrationality are all examples (§2.1, pp. 4–5). What does standard BT preference learning then estimate?
- **Approach.** **[Paper]** Utilities are $u(a,z)$, with hidden $z\sim\mathcal D_z$ and comparison probability $p_{u,\mathcal D_z}(a,b)=\mathbb E_z[O_u(a,b,z)]$ (eq. 4). BT-MLE is studied in the infinite-data limit, *assuming pairs are elicited uniformly at random* (p. 4, eq. 2), with optional $\ell_2$ regularisation (eq. 3). Results are then read through social choice theory (§3.2, App. B). The paper proposes DPL, which outputs a distribution of utilities per alternative (mean–variance or categorical), and an $r^2$ statistic for how much variation hidden context explains (§4).
- **Main findings and results.** **[Paper]** **Theorem 3.1** (p. 6): BT preference learning ranks alternatives by **Borda count**, $BC(a)=\frac1{|\mathcal A|}\sum_b p_{u,\mathcal D_z}(a,b)$, i.e. the average probability of beating a uniformly chosen alternative. **Theorem 3.2**: if the hidden-context noise is i.i.d. across alternatives (with a mild support condition), the BT ranking matches expected utility $\bar u$. **Proposition 3.3**: it can disagree with $\bar u$ even when every pairwise majority agrees with $\bar u$ (a 3-alternative example in App. A.5). **Theorem 3.4** (p. 7): from comparisons alone, $\bar u$ is not identifiable even up to monotone transformation. **Proposition 3.5**: $BC(a)=\mathbb E_z[F_{u|z}(u(a,z))]$, so Borda underweights extreme utilities. App. B.3: Borda is the only scoring rule that can be represented from pairwise proportions (Cor. B.8); Copeland and maximin can too (Prop. B.9). Experiments: on HH-RLHF, a reward model trained on combined helpful and harmless data has a 25.1% jailbreak rate, while risk-averse categorical DPL (0.01-quantile) has 13.4% at similar helpfulness accuracy (Table 1a, p. 10). $r^2$ falls when objectives are mixed (Table 1b).
- **Key assumptions.** **[Paper]** Infinite data, uniform pair design, optimisation over all functions on a finite set of alternatives (p. 4); $\lambda>0$ or existence of an unregularised minimiser (App. A.3). The LLM experiment relabels half of HH-RLHF with GPT-3.5 to stop the objective being inferred from the prompt (App. C.1).
- **Relation to the other papers.** **[Interpretation]** This is the closest existing result to ask (a). Replace "alternatives" with "LLMs" and "hidden context" with "voter group". Theorem 3.1 then says the Arena's uniform-over-pairs population BT score (Chiang et al., eq. 3) orders models by the Borda count of the *voter-averaged* win matrix. The theorem needs a uniform pair design, and Arena achieves exactly that target through reweighting (eq. 7). It does *not* cover cases where the voter mix differs across pairs, ties, or finite-sample inference. Those gaps are where the dissertation could add something.

### 2.4 The PRISM Alignment Dataset (Kirk, Whitefield, Röttger, Bean, Margatina, Ciro, Mosquera, Bartolo, Williams, He, Vidgen, Hale, 2024)
NeurIPS 2024 Datasets and Benchmarks Track; arXiv:2404.16019v2 [cs.CL], 3 December 2024. 107 pages: main text pp. 1–10, then appendices A–V.

- **Problem.** **[Paper]** Human-feedback datasets rely on narrow crowds, record few annotator details and aggregate indiscriminately, so hidden annotator context gets treated as universal (§1, pp. 1–2).
- **Approach.** **[Paper]** 1,500 English-speaking Prolific participants from 75 birth countries (38 countries of residence) complete a survey: demographics, LLM familiarity, stated preferences, a self-written "constitution" and a self-description (§2.1). They then hold 8,011 live conversations with 21 LLMs (§2.2, Fig. 1). Each participant chooses a conversation type: **unguided**, **values guided** or **controversy guided**, two of each. In the opening turn up to four randomly chosen models respond, and each response gets a 1–100 rating on a visual analogue scale. Later turns lock in the top-rated model. Sampling (§2.3) has two parts: census-representative UK and US samples (age, ethnicity, gender), and region-balanced samples from 33 other country studies. Models are told to answer in at most 50 words.
- **Main findings and results.** **[Paper]** *Case Study I* (§3.1): conversation type strongly shapes topics. Identity factors predict topic only modestly (11% of coefficients significant). *Case Study II* (§3.2, Fig. 4; App. T): ratings are converted to pairwise battles (tie threshold 5) and aggregated by Pairwise Rank Centrality. Rankings are "sensitive to idiosyncratic, contextual, and group-wise variance". With 100 people the top 10 are nearly indistinguishable. Relative to the overall rank, palm-2 drops 4 places for US participants, llama-7b drops 7 in Asia and mistral-7b gains 7 in Africa. zephyr-7b is strong on controversy prompts but not unguided ones, and claude-2 shows the opposite. Against LMSYS/Arena battles (App. T.9), GPT models do significantly worse in PRISM and open models such as zephyr-7b do better (bootstrap CIs). Response formatting and refusals explain part of the score differences, though $R^2=0.06$ is low (App. T.8). *Case Study III* (§3.3, Fig. 5; App. U): when a model is selected by a sub-sample, smaller or demographically narrow samples tend to produce lower welfare for out-groups. No model is chosen by a majority: the maximum MEAN CHOICE is 45% in the US.
- **Key assumptions.** **[Paper]** Unseen ratings are treated as missing at random (§3.2). Cardinal scores are used across people even though the authors note interpersonal comparability problems (§5; App. B.10, App. T.1). Robustness to aggregation function, tie threshold, subset and regularisation is in App. T.3–T.6: head and tail are stable, mid-table positions move.
- **Relation to the other papers.** **[Interpretation]** PRISM is the "dataset in which the voter groups are known". It also provides direct empirical evidence for ask (b): group-wise rank shifts of up to 7 places. Its aggregation rule (rank centrality) is one of the ranking functionals in Frauen et al. (eq. 10). Its comparison with LMSYS is an informal version of "does the ranking carry over to another population?". Unlike Arena, PRISM collects ratings, not binary votes, and shows up to four models at once. A pairwise version has to be constructed, and the construction choices matter (App. T.1, T.4).

### 2.5 Nonparametric LLM Evaluation from Preference Data (Frauen, Deviyani, van der Schaar, Feuerriegel, 2026)
ICML 2026 (PMLR 306); arXiv:2601.21816v2 [cs.LG], 6 June 2026.

- **Problem.** **[Paper]** Parametric BT can be biased under misspecification and does not combine with flexible ML estimators or LLM judges while keeping valid inference (§1, pp. 1–2; §4.1).
- **Approach.** **[Paper]** Data are $O=(X,S,\tilde Y)$: context $X$ (e.g. the prompt), selection indicators $S_{jk}$ and categorical preference labels $Y_{jk}$, allowing ties (§3). The nuisances are $\mu_{jkc}(x)=P(Y_{jkc}=1\mid X=x)$ (eq. 1) and the selection propensities $\pi_{jk}(x)$ (eq. 2). The target is a **generalised average ranking score (GARS)**, $\theta=\mathbb E[F(\mu(X))]$ for a known $F$ (eq. 8, p. 4). Examples are a BT projection (eq. 7), Borda/average win rate (eq. 9) and rank centrality (eq. 10). **Theorem 5.1** (p. 5) gives the efficient influence function, an AIPW-style correction weighted by $S_{jk}/\pi_{jk}$ and the Jacobian of $F$ (eq. 13), and a one-step debiased estimator (eq. 14) with $\sqrt n$-asymptotic normality (eq. 15). It uses cross-fitting (§5.3), a confidence ellipsoid (eq. 17) and simultaneous intervals (Gaussian-max or Bonferroni, App. I). **Theorem 6.2** (p. 7) gives an A-optimal labelling policy under a budget.
- **Main findings and results.** **[Paper]** In simulations, the debiased estimators reach near-nominal coverage while plug-in coverage is "invalid" (Table 1, e.g. Borda, $n=1000$: 0.94 vs 0.17). External judges reduce error (Fig. 4a). Under BT misspecification the debiased projection estimator beats a BT-restricted one (Fig. 4b). On Chatbot Arena data (32,980 prompts, 20 models; §7.2, App. M), Borda, BT and rank-centrality rankings broadly agree with a baseline ranking but differ from each other. BT deviates most, and the plug-in intervals "collapse" (Fig. 3, p. 8).
- **Key assumptions.** **[Paper]** Positivity, missing at random ($Y\perp S\mid X$), i.i.d. sampling, a smooth $F$, and product-rate conditions on nuisance estimators (App. B.1). The authors list "lack of overlap", including "annotator subpopulations [with] very small selection probabilities", and missing-at-random violations as key limitations. They suggest partial identification, yielding "uncertainty sets or bounds for ranking scores", as a future direction (§8, p. 9).
- **Relation to the other papers.** **[Interpretation]** Two features matter most for this project. First, a GARS averages $F$ over the context distribution, so if a voter segment $G$ is part of $X$, then $\theta(w)=\sum_g w_g\,\mathbb E[F(\mu(X))\mid G=g]$ is **linear in the mixing weights $w$**, whatever $F$ is. Arena's pooled BT (Chiang et al., eq. 3) instead applies the BT map *after* averaging over voters, which is nonlinear in $w$. Second, the inference machinery gives jointly normal estimates of segment-level scores, which a "for every plausible mix" statement needs. The future-work paragraph is close to asks (b)–(c).

## 3. How the papers fit together

**Common thread.** **[Interpretation]** All five papers turn pairwise human judgements into a ranking. Each one, explicitly or not, has to choose (i) an *estimand*, meaning which functional of the comparison distribution is "the ranking", and (ii) whose comparisons, on which prompts, define that distribution. Chiang et al. and Ameli et al. fix (ii) implicitly, as "whoever votes on Arena". DPL and PRISM show that (ii) matters. Frauen et al. make (i) explicit and flexible.

**Axes along which they differ.**

| Paper | Ranking functional | Heterogeneity modelled? | Inference | Data |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chiang et al. | Population BT, uniform over pairs (eq. 3); nonparametric BT (App. B) | No (pooled) | Sandwich CLT, simultaneous chi-squared set | Arena votes |
| Ameli et al. | BT / Rao–Kupper / Davidson, with factored ties and Thurstonian covariance | Only via the random-score covariance | MLE; identifiability analysis; no CIs reported | Arena aggregated counts |
| DPL | BT ⇒ Borda count (Thm 3.1); DPL distributions | Yes, hidden context $z$ | None (population-level theory) | Synthetic; HH-RLHF |
| PRISM | Pairwise Rank Centrality (others in App. T.3) | Yes, observed demographics and conversation type | Bootstrap | PRISM; LMSYS battles |
| Frauen et al. | Any GARS $\mathbb E[F(\mu(X))]$: BT projection, Borda, rank centrality | Through context $X$ (prompt features) | EIF-based debiased CLT, simultaneous CIs | Synthetic; Arena; MT-Bench |

```
            estimand question                      population question
   Ameli (which likelihood?)  ─┐         ┌─ DPL (pooled BT = Borda of mixture)
   Frauen (which functional?) ─┼─ Arena ─┼─ PRISM (ranks move with group / topic)
   Chiang (pop. BT, uniform)  ─┘         └─ (this project: bounds + uniform-in-mix CIs)
```

**Settled (within these papers).** **[Paper]** Pooled BT on a uniform design ranks by Borda count of the mixture (DPL Thm 3.1). Comparisons alone cannot identify mean utility (DPL Thm 3.4). Absolute BT scores are not identifiable; only differences are (Ameli App. B). Sandwich or EIF-based intervals give valid coverage for misspecified or nonparametric targets (Chiang §5, App. A; Frauen Thm 5.1). Empirically, rankings differ by topic (Chiang Table 2), by voter group and conversation type (PRISM Fig. 4), by aggregation rule (PRISM App. T.3; Frauen Fig. 3) and by model specification (Ameli §4.1).

**Unsettled.** **[Interpretation]** (1) What pooled BT estimates when the voter mix differs *across pairs*, e.g. because newer models are shown during periods with different traffic. Theorem 3.1 assumes a common mixture and a uniform design. (2) Quantitative bounds on rank changes as the mix varies over a set. PRISM reports observed shifts for a few subgroups but no bounds. (3) Confidence statements that hold uniformly over a set of mixes. No paper provides them. Frauen et al. name partial identification as open (§8). (4) How to treat segments, such as language or prompt type, that are attributes of the *prompt* rather than of the *voter*.

## 4. Tie-in to the dissertation

### 4.1 What the proposed directions mean in practice

**(a) Characterise what the pooled ranking estimates under heterogeneous voters.** **[Interpretation]** Set up $G$ voter groups with weights $w\in\Delta_G$ (the probability simplex). Give each group its own win probabilities $p_g(i,j)$, perhaps from its own BT model, and its own pair-exposure distribution $q_g(i,j)$. The Arena target (Chiang eq. 3) is the minimiser $\xi^*(w,q)$ of $\sum_{i<j}\sum_g w_g q_g(i,j)\,\mathrm{CE}\big(p_g(i,j),\sigma(\xi_j-\xi_i)\big)$. Its first-order condition is a set of balance equations: fitted win probabilities equal exposure-weighted average group win rates. Concretely, the work would involve:
- re-deriving DPL Thm 3.1 in Arena notation, showing that with uniform, group-independent exposure the pooled ranking is the Borda ranking of $\bar p(w)=\sum_g w_g p_g$;
- characterising what changes when exposure depends on the group or pair, and when ties are present;
- producing explicit counterexamples in which the pooled ranking matches no group's ranking (with intransitive mixtures in the spirit of DPL Prop. 3.3);
- using misspecified-MLE theory (Huber/White, as Chiang et al. invoke on p. 4) to give the sampling distribution around $\xi^*$.

**(b) Bounds on rank change under different or uncertain mixes.** **[Interpretation]** Define a set $\mathcal W\subseteq\Delta_G$ of plausible mixes: e.g. a box around observed shares, a divergence ball around the empirical mix, or census targets as in PRISM App. L. Then study $\min_{w\in\mathcal W}$ and $\max_{w\in\mathcal W}$ of $\mathrm{rank}_A(w)$. For linear-in-$w$ estimands (Borda scores; any GARS with the segment inside $X$), each pairwise comparison $A$ vs $B$ is a linear programme over $\mathcal W$: is there a $w$ with $\theta_B(w)>\theta_A(w)$? Best and worst ranks then follow by combining these, which may need a mixed-integer programme, because one $w$ must flip several comparisons at once. For the pooled BT estimand $\xi^*(w)$, the map is nonlinear. One route is sensitivity analysis via the implicit function theorem on the balance equations; another is bounding it with Borda-type surrogates.

**(c) "A above B for every plausible mix" confidence statements.** **[Interpretation]** With jointly asymptotically normal segment-level estimates $\hat\Delta_g$ of $\theta_A^g-\theta_B^g$ (from stratified MLE, a sandwich variance or Frauen's EIF estimator), test $H_0:\min_{w\in\mathcal W}w^\top\Delta\le 0$. A conservative version checks whether $\min_{w\in\mathcal W}\min_{\Delta\in C_{1-\alpha}}w^\top\Delta>0$ for a simultaneous confidence set $C_{1-\alpha}$ (cf. Frauen eq. 17, App. I; Chiang eq. 8). When $\mathcal W$ is the whole simplex this reduces to "A beats B in every group". Sharper, non-conservative versions and multiplicity control across all model pairs are the methodological content.

**Simulation.** **[Interpretation]** Generate groups with distinct BT (or non-BT) preferences, Arena-like adaptive exposure and ties. Check that the characterisation in (a) is right, that the bounds in (b) are attained, and that the coverage in (c) is nominal as $n$ grows. Include stress cases: small segments, a group-dependent pair exposure, intransitive groups.

**Real data, observable segments.** **[Interpretation]** Use publicly released Arena votes. Treat language (detected from the prompt) and prompt type (a topic cluster, as in Chiang §6.1) as segments, estimate segment-level scores, and report which top-10 orderings survive every plausible mix. See §6 below for what the papers do and do not confirm about the fields.

**Known groups.** **[Interpretation]** Use PRISM. Build pairwise battles from opening-turn ratings (App. T.1), use demographics, birth region or conversation type as groups, and check whether bounds computed from coarse segments contain the rankings of the actual subgroups. The census-representative UK/US samples (§2.3, App. L) give a natural "target population" mix.

### 4.2 What a dissertation would plausibly involve

**[Interpretation]** Shape 1, theory first (my best guess at what the brief intends):
1. A chapter formalising the mixture model and proving a characterisation of the pooled BT estimand: an extension of DPL Thm 3.1 to group-dependent exposure and ties, with counterexamples.
2. A chapter on robustness sets $\mathcal W$, with exact rank bounds for linear estimands and approximate ones for pooled BT.
3. A chapter on uniform-in-$w$ confidence statements with a coverage proof under standard M-estimation conditions.
4. Simulations.
5. Arena and PRISM case studies.

Roughly 40% theory, 25% simulation, 35% data.

**[Interpretation]** Shape 2, methods and empirics first: take the estimand result largely as given from DPL. Build a practical "robust leaderboard" procedure: segment-stratified BT or Borda estimates with sandwich covariance, LP-based rank intervals over $\mathcal W$, and simultaneous tests. Validate it carefully on PRISM, where the true groups are known, and apply it to Arena. This version needs less new proof and more careful software and data work. It may also optionally use Frauen-style debiasing if prompt-level covariates are used.

### 4.3 Open questions

- **[Paper]** Chiang et al. expect a biased user distribution ("LLM hobbyists and researchers") and a prompt mix that may not reflect production use (§8, p. 8). They plan topic-specific leaderboards (§8). **[Interpretation]** A topic leaderboard is a segment-level estimand, which ask (b) would relate back to the pooled one.
- **[Paper]** Frauen et al.: lack of overlap when "annotator subpopulations have very small selection probabilities"; reliance on missing at random; partial identification to get "uncertainty sets or bounds for ranking scores" as a future direction (§8, p. 9).
- **[Paper]** DPL: "Under what theoretical conditions can DPL accurately estimate the distribution of utilities…?" and which other failures hidden context causes (§6, p. 12). Annotators have an incentive to misreport under Borda aggregation (§3.2).
- **[Paper]** PRISM: the "curse of dimensionality (or intersectionality)": many possible groupings, too little power for sparse ones, and coarse categories such as "Other" with limited meaning (App. B.10). Cardinal ratings are not interpersonally comparable; normalising may remove real between-group signal (App. T.1). Future work should separate style from content and add participant, model and conversation fixed effects (§3.2).
- **[Paper]** Ameli et al.: whether MM algorithms extend to their generalised models is open (App. D.1).

### Data
**Chatbot Arena / LMSYS votes (the "publicly released leaderboard votes").**
- **[Paper]** Chiang et al. say they "will publicly release a human preference dataset with over 100K pairwise votes" (§1, p. 2; §9). Users accept terms of use giving "consent to release the data publicly" (§3.1, p. 3). Each data point "includes multi-turn conversations between the user and two LLMs, and a vote" (§3.2, p. 3). Votes can be model A, model B, tie, or "both are bad" (§3.1). Conversations span more than 100 languages (77% English, 5% Chinese) (§3.2). Prompt topics were derived *post hoc* by clustering (§6.1).
- **[Paper]** The paper gives **no dataset URL and no field list**. It does not say whether a language label, topic label, user identifier or IP-derived identifier is included in the release. IP addresses are used internally for anomaly detection (§5.1).
- **[Paper]** Other papers describe released Arena-type data as follows:
  - Frauen et al. use "Chatbot Arena conversations" from Zheng et al. (2023), described as "public". Each row has a prompt of up to three turns, `model_a`, `model_b`, and a winner in {model_a, model_b, tie, tie (bothbad)}, keyed by `question_id`. That gives 32,980 contexts after deduplication and 20 models (App. M.1, p. 42).
  - PRISM downloads "LMSYS battles": 184,610 battles over 54 models, with a footnote pointing to huggingface.co/spaces/lmsys/chatbot-arena-leaderboard (App. T.9).
  - Ameli et al. use a September 2024 snapshot as aggregated win/tie matrices: 129 models, 1,374,996 comparisons (§3).
- **[Interpretation]** For the brief's "language" segment, language would probably have to be detected from the prompt text, unless a release includes a label; the papers do not confirm either way. "Prompt type" would come from topic clustering in the style of Chiang §6.1, or from a category label if one exists. Neither the language field nor the prompt-type field is confirmed by the supplied papers, so check this before the meeting (Q2 below). Aggregated count matrices, such as `leaderbot`'s, discard prompts and so cannot be segmented.

**PRISM (the "dataset in which the voter groups are known").**
- **[Paper]** Access: GitHub (above) and Hugging Face at huggingface.co/datasets/HannahRoseKirk/prism-alignment, DOI 10.57967/hf/2113 (abstract; App. A).
- **[Paper]** Licence: human-written text is CC-BY-4.0; model responses are CC-BY-NC-4.0 and subject to model providers' licences (App. C.2). The terms forbid de-anonymisation (App. C.1). There will be no further updates (App. C.3).
- **[Paper]** Files (App. A):
  - `survey.jsonl`: one row per participant, keyed by `user_id`.
  - `conversations.jsonl`: one row per conversation, with `conversation_type`, `opening_prompt`, and a `conversation_history` that holds per-response `model_name`, `model_provider`, `score` (1–100), `if_chosen` and `within_turn_id`, plus performance attributes and open feedback.
  - `utterances.jsonl`: one scored utterance per row.
  - `metadata.jsonl`: language detection, PII and moderation flags.
- **[Paper]** Survey variables (codebook App. V.1) include age, gender, education, employment, marital status, ethnicity (self-described, categorised, simplified), religion (likewise), English proficiency, birth and residence country and subregion, LLM familiarity and usage, stated preferences, `system_string` and `self_description`. There is an `included_in_balanced_subset` flag (App. K) and census-rebalancing flags for UK/US (App. K–L).

There are 42,306 battles over 21 models once converted to pairs (App. T.9). 99% of text is English (App. B.2), so **language cannot serve as a segment in PRISM**. Conversation type (unguided / values / controversy) is a recorded, participant-chosen prompt-type variable. **[Interpretation]** That makes it a direct analogue of the brief's "prompt type" segment.

**Other datasets.** **[Paper]** HH-RLHF, with helpful versus harmless subsets as a known hidden context, plus 187 jailbreak response pairs (DPL §5, App. C.3). MT-Bench human judgements: 2,396 comparisons, 6 models (Frauen App. M.1). Both are described as public or come with released code.

### Compute
- **[Paper]** Ameli et al.: each of 30 model variants was fitted on an AMD EPYC 7543 32-core CPU. Times in Table D.1 range from 0.0 to 817.3; the unit is not stated, but it is presumably seconds (App. D.1). Frauen et al.: LightGBM nuisance models with randomised hyperparameter search; no hardware is reported (App. K, M). DPL: LoRA fine-tuning of Llama-2-7B reward models; no GPU type or run time is reported (App. C.2). Chiang et al.: no compute figures. PRISM: model responses came via APIs; no compute figures for the analyses.
- **[Interpretation]** The project is light on compute. BT or Borda fits with tens of models and up to around 1–2 million votes, stratified by tens of segments, run in seconds to minutes on a laptop. So do bootstraps of a few hundred to a thousand replicates and LP or MIP rank bounds. Simulations are embarrassingly parallel and fit easily on a laptop or a small university-cluster allocation. Text embeddings for prompt-type clustering of Arena prompts are the heaviest step. A small sentence-transformer on CPU, or a short GPU session, is enough. Nothing in the brief requires training LLMs, so DPL's GPU fine-tuning would only be needed if the reward-model side were added, which I would not recommend.

## 7. Questions for the proposal meeting

1. **Which pooled estimand is "the leaderboard"?** Chiang et al.'s uniform-over-pairs population BT (eq. 3), plain BT on observed frequencies, Elo, or a Borda/win-rate score? The answer to ask (a) differs, and linear-in-mix estimands make (b)–(c) much easier than BT.
2. **Which public vote release should be used, and what fields does it have?** The supplied papers do not confirm language labels, topic or category labels, or voter identifiers. If there are no voter IDs, within-voter dependence and repeat voting cannot be modelled.
3. **Are "language" and "prompt type" meant as proxies for voter groups, or as segments in their own right?** They are attributes of the prompt, not of the voter. That changes the interpretation of "user mix" (a mix of users versus a mix of tasks).
4. **How should the set of "plausible mixes" be defined?** A box around observed shares, a divergence ball, external targets such as census shares (PRISM App. L), or something supervisor-specified? The bounds and confidence statements depend entirely on this choice.
5. **Should the theory allow the user mix to differ across model pairs** (time-varying traffic, adaptive pair sampling as in Chiang eq. 9)? This seems central to the brief's "depends on which pairs happen to be shown", but it complicates the analysis considerably.
6. **Observed segments only, or also latent (unobserved) groups?** With latent groups (as in DPL's hidden context), identifiability becomes the main issue (DPL Thm 3.4).
7. **How should ties and "both bad" votes be treated?** Options: as half-wins (Chiang), with a Rao–Kupper or Davidson model (Ameli), or as extra categories (Frauen App. C). About 20% of Arena outcomes are ties.
8. **How should PRISM's cardinal ratings be converted to pairwise comparisons?** The tie threshold and normalisation matter (PRISM App. T.1, T.4). Is PRISM a test of the method (known groups) or a second application?
9. **Asymptotic or finite-sample guarantees for the "every plausible mix" statements?** Do you expect a formal coverage theorem, or are simulation-validated procedures enough?
10. **Is Frauen et al.'s debiased-ML framework expected to be used, or is it background?** It adds prompt-level covariates and efficiency, but also a substantial learning cost.
11. **What balance of proof, simulation and data analysis does the supervisor expect, and is a reusable software artefact** (e.g. a robust-leaderboard function) **valued?**
12. **What counts as success on the real data?** For example: identifying which top-k orderings on the public leaderboard are robust to plausible user mixes, or showing that some published orderings are not.
