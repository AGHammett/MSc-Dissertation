# Score-based Generative Modelling with Diffusion Processes: Plain summary

**Supervisor:** Dr Oliver Feng

## 1. The domain

Generative modelling is the part of machine learning that tries to produce new, artificial data that looks as if it came from the same distribution as a training set. Typical examples are new face images that resemble a database of photos, or new audio that resembles recorded speech. Over the last few years one approach has come to dominate image generation: *diffusion models*, also called *score-based generative models*. They sit behind well-known image generators. Statisticians are interested because the method rests on probability theory (stochastic processes and stochastic differential equations, the "continuous-time random walks" you meet in an Itô calculus course), and because nobody yet fully understands *why* it works so well.

## 2. The problem

The idea is to take real data and gradually add random noise until it is pure noise. Then you learn to run that process backwards. Running it backwards needs one key ingredient at every noise level: the *score*, which is the direction in which the probability density of the noisy data increases fastest. If you know the score, you can push random noise step by step towards realistic data. The score is unknown and has to be estimated from data; the general technique for this is called *score matching*. Two kinds of question are open:

- **How well can you estimate the score** from a finite sample, and is there a best possible accuracy?
- **How many steps does the backwards process need**, and how do errors in the estimated score turn into errors in the generated samples?

## 3. What's been done

- **Song and Ermon (2019)** introduced a practical version of the method. A neural network learns the score at many noise levels at once. Samples are then generated by *Langevin dynamics*, a noisy gradient-ascent procedure that only needs the score, run from high noise down to low noise. They showed why the naive version fails: real data sits on thin, low-dimensional structures, and the score is poorly learnt in empty regions. Adding noise fixes both problems. They obtained image quality competitive with the best methods of the time.
- **Li, Wei, Chen and Chi (2024)** assume a reasonably accurate score has been learnt and ask how many steps two popular backwards samplers need to get close to the true distribution. The deterministic sampler improves at a rate of roughly one over the number of steps; the random sampler at roughly one over its square root. They also propose faster variants. Their proofs deliberately avoid stochastic calculus and work directly with the step-by-step process.
- **Dou, Kotekal, Xu and Zhou (2024)** treat score estimation as a classical statistics problem in one dimension. They find the best achievable accuracy for every noise level, with matching upper and lower bounds, using kernel smoothing rather than neural networks. They then show that the resulting generative model estimates the data distribution as well as any method possibly could, without some ad hoc tricks that earlier theory needed.

## 4. What the project would do

The core requirement is a careful mathematical write-up of score-based modelling: score matching, the forward "noising" process and its reverse, and how errors in the score affect the samples. After that the brief offers a choice:

- **apply or adapt** the method to a generative task of your choice, on simulated or real data;
- **extend score matching to other kinds of data**, for example data confined to a bounded region or a curved surface (the supplied papers say little about this);
- **survey and possibly extend** the recent convergence and accuracy results, for instance by checking them in simulations, working out a variant, or combining the two theory papers' results.

## 5. What working on it would feel like

I think this depends heavily on the route chosen at the meeting. A theory-leaning version might be roughly 55% reading and writing proofs, 25% learning background (nonparametric statistics, lower-bound techniques, more stochastic calculus) and 20% coding small simulations. An applied version might be closer to 35% exposition, 50% coding and experiments, and 15% analysis. A typical theory week would mean working through a few pages of a long proof with pen and paper, then writing up a cleaner version, perhaps checking a predicted rate with a short Python simulation. A typical applied week would mean training a small score network on toy or small image data, comparing samplers, and diagnosing why one fails. Your Itô calculus module will be used directly, but the two theory papers are long (around 70 pages each) and technical, so the reading needs to be scoped carefully.
